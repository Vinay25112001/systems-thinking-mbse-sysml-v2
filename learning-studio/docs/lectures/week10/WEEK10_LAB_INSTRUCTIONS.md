## Engineering method

1. **Functions with their items.** Each Week 4 function becomes an action usage with its ID as short name. Give it an in parameter for every item it consumes and an out parameter for every item it produces, using your information items; add an item where a flow has none (an information item takes the next ID; an energy or material item is named). A function with no input, or no output, or neither, needs a disposition saying why. A team of three gives items to the functions its chosen scenario uses; the others may wait for the final model. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 6 ‘Functions and the items they exchange’; Session 1, slide 11 ‘Functions as actions: parameters and items’.)*
2. **Capabilities.** Each capability becomes an action definition; each function, and each requirement Week 9 left tracing to a capability, gets a dependency to it, from the dependent element to the capability. A function that realizes no capability, or a capability no function realizes, needs a disposition. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 9 ‘Capabilities, functions and the links Week 9 left waiting’.)*
3. **Logical architecture and allocation.** Each logical element becomes a part usage inside the logical architecture, performing the functions allocated to it; a Week 9 placeholder subject of the same name is reused, and any other placeholder is linked to the element it stands for. Each requirement allocated to a function in Week 4 is written as an allocation from the requirement to the function; an unallocated system requirement needs a disposition. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 8 ‘Logical decomposition: part definitions and part usages’; Session 1, slide 12 ‘Allocating functions to logical elements: perform and allocate’.)*
4. **One scenario as an action flow.** Choose the scenario with the most alternates (a team of three: any scenario with at least one alternate) and fill the mapping table: for each step, the action’s name and the function it is an occurrence of (none for a step another party performs); for each alternate, its action, a guard naming its condition, and where the decision sits: where the condition becomes known, before the step it branches from (the alternate then replaces the step) or after it. The tool writes the successions, each decision with an else, a merge where an alternate rejoins and done where it ends, and an item flow wherever an earlier step’s function produces what a later step’s function consumes: at least 1 decision and 1 item flow. A decision can sit only before or after a whole step: if a condition becomes known in the middle of a step, split the step in your Week 2 record and import again. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 16 ‘Choices: conditional successions, decide and merge’; Session 1, slide 20 ‘Worked example II: SCN-002 from ConOps table to action flow’.)*
5. **Use cases.** Every scenario becomes a use case with the enterprise as subject, an actor for each external party typed by your stakeholder or external-system definitions, and the success end state as objective. Type the actor IDs where the name match is wrong, or record why an actor is not modeled. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 12 ‘Use cases: subject, actors, objective’; Session 2, slide 14 ‘Worked example IV: five scenarios as use cases’.)*
6. **Operating states.** Define the operating states of your principal element: at least 4 states, 5 transitions with a trigger each and 1 guard (a team of three: 3 states and 4 transitions), an initial state, entry or do functions where a state performs one, and the part that exhibits the states. Every state must be reachable from the initial state. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 4 ‘State definitions and the initial state’; Session 2, slide 9 ‘Worked example III: the aircraft’s operating states’.)*
7. **Behavior review.** Read the model against the 8 checks of the course checklist and the cross-check of flow and states; record for each check whether the model meets it, with how you checked, or the finding and what you will do. Give every behavior finding a disposition: an open item with an owner and a week, or why no change is needed. A disposition cannot claim a fix: change the choice or the record and import again. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 15 ‘Cross-checking the flow and the states’; Session 2, slide 17 ‘The behavior review checklist’.)*
8. **Checker to zero errors.** Run model v0.3 through the course-designated checker and correct it until the checker reports no errors; explain every warning in the log. A PASS shows the file parsed, every name resolved and no rule fired; it does not show that a flow follows the ConOps, that a guard means its condition, or that the states are complete. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 22 ‘What the checker checks, and what it cannot’; Session 2, slide 16 ‘Common errors in behavior models’.)*

## The behavior review checklist (course convention)

| Check | What the tool shows |
|---|---|
| Every ConOps step and every alternate of the chosen scenario has a node in the flow, in ConOps order | The mapping table lists each step and alternate with its node. |
| Each alternate is entered from its ConOps step and either ends or rejoins where the ConOps says | The mapping table shows the rejoin step or “ends” for each alternate. |
| Every decision branch has a guard, an else covers the remaining cases, and the guards say what the ConOps condition says | The generator writes a guard per alternate and an else; whether the guard means the condition is yours to judge. |
| Every item flow joins a step that produces the item to a later step that consumes it | Item flows are computed from the functions’ out and in items. |
| Every function has its inputs and outputs and at least one performer | Gaps list functions with no input, no output or no performer. |
| Every state is reachable from the initial state, and every transition has a trigger | Gaps list unreachable states; the generator writes a trigger on every transition. |
| Flow and states agree: each step that changes the element’s condition has a transition, and each transition is caused by a step of some scenario | The cross-check table pairs steps and transitions; the pairing is yours to judge. |
| Every system requirement is allocated to at least one function, or has a disposition | Trace findings list unallocated system requirements. |

## The lab tool

The lab tool reads three files: your Week 9 model v0.2 (.sysml), your Week 4 model inputs and your Week 2 model inputs (.json). It refuses AeroLink files, files of another team, a Week 8 model and files whose packages are missing, each with the reason. For every function you enter the items it consumes and produces and choose its performer; you add functions and items where a scenario needs them, with IDs that continue after the highest your files have used and are never reused. You link the Week 9 placeholder subjects, map one scenario step by step, type the use-case actors and define one set of operating states. The tool writes model v0.3: it fills the Logical package (capabilities, items, functions, the logical architecture, requirement allocations) and the Behavior package (a use case for every scenario, the chosen scenario as an action flow, the state definition) and adds the exhibit to the part you choose. It computes the behavior audit and the cross-check, keeps the review checklist and shows a readiness list with the reason for every item still open. Only a real defect keeps an item out of the model (a reference to something that is not in your files would not resolve). Its checks are course acceptance checks: whether the model is valid is decided by the course-designated checker, and a checker PASS does not show that a flow follows the ConOps. A model v0.3 exported from the tool can be imported again; the tool replaces what it generated, so keep hand edits in a separate file until the last export. Everything is saved in your browser only: export after every working session, and share the workspace file with teammates.
