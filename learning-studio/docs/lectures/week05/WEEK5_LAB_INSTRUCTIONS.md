## Engineering method

1. **Component breakdown and responsibility cards.** Take each Week 4 physical element as level 1 and write its responsibility card: what it is responsible for, the organization that owns it, and the functions your Week 4 allocation already places on it. Break an element down into level-2 components where that helps define its interfaces; a component may be hardware, software, data, people (a role or team), procedure or process, facility, a combination, and one word is enough for its name. Each component refines its parent’s allocation and never contradicts it. Hard gates for every component: a name (one word is enough); placed under a Week 4 physical element that exists; its kind: hardware, software, data, people, procedure, facility or a combination; its responsibility: an action and what it acts on, at least two words (“Charges aircraft”). For every card: the responsibility of the physical element: an action and what it acts on, at least two words (“Raises alarms”); the organization that owns it (one word is enough). Advisories: five plus or minus two components per level is the usual guideline; a physical element broken into a single component: one child is a rename, not a breakdown. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 4 ‘System element and component’; Session 1, slide 5 ‘Layers, and five plus or minus two per level’; Session 1, slide 6 ‘System breakdown structure and work breakdown structure’; Session 1, slide 7 ‘Logical and physical: which elements we break down’; Session 1, slide 8 ‘Worked example I: AeroLink’s component breakdown’; Session 1, slide 9 ‘Responsibility cards: one per element’.)*
2. **Identify and type the interfaces.** For every pair of elements ask whether anything crosses between them. Type each interface: physical (energy, material, mechanical load (things that move or bind)); information (data, over a named medium); organizational (nothing: it records an agreement, a procedure or a responsibility between owners). An interface with an external end is external; an internal interface carried over a network, link or service your enterprise may not own is a hidden external interface, so record it. Include every external element: give it an interface, or record the reason it has none. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 10 ‘What an interface is’; Session 1, slide 11 ‘Typing interfaces: physical, information, organizational; external flagged’; Session 1, slide 12 ‘External and hidden external interfaces’.)*
3. **The N² chart.** Put the physical elements and the external elements on the diagonal; each cell lists the interfaces joining its row and column elements (the chart is symmetric). Read the degree of each element (its interface count): the degree sum is twice the number of interfaces, a high degree marks a hub to protect and integrate early, and an empty row or column is an isolated element to explain. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 13 ‘The N² chart: how it is built’; Session 1, slide 14 ‘Worked example II: AeroLink’s N² from the fourteen interfaces’; Session 1, slide 15 ‘Reading the N²: the hub, the empty rows, the sum’.)*
4. **The crossing-flow check.** For every Week 4 information item, take every producer and consumer pair on different elements (an AI capability stands at its host element) and find the information interface it rides, directly or by a relay of interfaces you record in order. A flow with no interface has nothing to verify: add the interface, or record the relay. Rule: every Week 4 information route between two different elements rides an information interface, directly or by a recorded relay. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 16 ‘Functional flows across the physical boundary’; Session 1, slide 17 ‘Answering the driving question: a new interface, chosen with reasons’.)*
5. **The interface definition table.** Describe every interface with the same attributes: ID and name; kind (external flagged); ends; what crosses; direction; medium; constraint and requirement; owner of each side; verification; protection; degraded mode. Hard gates: two different ends, each a physical element, a component or an external element in the record; its kind: physical, information or organizational; what crosses it, in words or as information item IDs; the direction: from which end to which, for what flows (an organizational interface needs none); the medium or carrier: connector, network, document or agreement; the owner of each side; if it crosses a trust boundary: its protection, or the reason it needs none (one word is enough: “TLS”, “Power only”); its degraded mode: what each side does when the interface fails, at least two words (“Fail safe”). Advisories: an organizational interface lists information items: an agreement carries no data, so add an information interface for the flow (course coverage rule); no constraint or requirement yet: write one when the interface carries something a requirement depends on; no verification named: every interface is verified during integration; the medium names radio, Wi-Fi, cellular, satellite, a VPN, the internet or another network, link or service outsiders can reach or you may not own: tick “An end accepts input from the public, or the medium is radio or a network we may not own” (it then crosses a trust boundary) and record the hidden external interface; an owner differs from the owner on that end’s responsibility card: if it is the same organization spelled differently (“Ops” and “Operations”), use one spelling, or the tool sees a false trust-boundary crossing; both ends are external elements: an interface between two outsiders is outside your system of interest; check that it belongs in your set; an external end with the same owner as the internal end: check the boundary. No interface of one kind: fine if your enterprise has none (a software-only service may have no physical interface), otherwise check. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 18 ‘The interface definition table’; Session 1, slide 19 ‘Worked example III: defining the fire alarm and incident-record interfaces’; Session 1, slide 21 ‘Modularity: maximum consistency inside, minimum interfaces outside’.)*
6. **Interface requirements in three steps.** First define the boundary (the two elements and the interface between them), then the interaction (what crosses, in which direction, over which medium), then write the requirement on it, one per external interface where you can. Each takes the next free system-requirement ID and keeps the Week 2 rules. Hard gates: exactly one “shall” (one requirement per statement); names the interface it governs, an interface in the record; a verification criterion: a value with its unit (“2 min”) or a TBD with an owner (“TBD (Ops)”); one token counts when it holds a number or a TBD, otherwise two words; a verification method: inspection, analysis, demonstration or test; a parent need, capability or requirement that exists in the record. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 20 ‘Interface requirements in three steps’; Session 1, slide 19 ‘Worked example III: defining the fire alarm and incident-record interfaces’.)*
7. **Trust boundaries, protection and degraded modes.** An interface crosses a trust boundary when one end is external, when its two sides have different owners, or when an end accepts input from the public or the medium is radio (including Wi-Fi and cellular) or a network you may not own (radio is a hidden external interface whoever owns the radios, R54 p.331). For each such interface state its protection, for example: authenticate the sender; integrity-check every message (detect alteration); encrypt personal or commercial data in transit; domain separation: no direct path from a public front end to a safety function; least privilege: the sender may write only what it owns; boundary enforcement: reject messages outside the agreed format or rate; a local interlock that acts without the interface; or the reason it needs none. For every interface state the degraded mode, what each side does when it fails, for example: fail safe: treat a missing status as the unsafe value (for example no-go); continue on the last valid value for a stated time, then stop; switch to a backup path or a manual procedure; stop the dependent function and tell its users. Link the interfaces to the risks in your register; at least one interface linked to a security or resilience risk in the register. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 6 ‘Interface security: trust boundaries’; Session 2, slide 7 ‘Protection at the interface: integrity, authentication, separation’; Session 2, slide 8 ‘Degraded modes at interfaces: what each side does when it fails’; Session 2, slide 9 ‘Worked example II: trust boundaries, protection and degraded modes on AeroLink’.)*
8. **Interface management.** Name the interface control working group (the organizations that own interfacing elements), the standards that apply or “none” with the reason, and the agreements between owners. Write at least one interface change request: the interface, the change, its impact found from the N² row and column of the interface’s ends, and the decision with the decider. Hard gates: the interface it changes, an interface in the record; the change: an action and what it acts on, at least two words (“Add encryption”); at least one affected interface or element found from the N² row and column, existing in the record; the decision (one word is enough: “Approved”) and who made it. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 2 ‘Why interfaces are managed, not just defined’; Session 2, slide 3 ‘Governance and the interface control working group’; Session 2, slide 4 ‘Standards and service-level agreements’; Session 2, slide 5 ‘Worked example I: one interface change, traced through the N²’.)*
9. **Audit, integration and coupling.** Audit the set: externals without an interface, information routes without an interface, interfaces without verification, constraint or protection, hidden external interfaces. Group the elements into integration aggregates that keep the interfaces between aggregates few, and read what the N² cannot tell you (how critical an interface is, or how much crosses it). List what is still open for Week 6. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 10 ‘Auditing the interface set: four lenses’; Session 2, slide 11 ‘Integration: aggregates from the N²’; Session 2, slide 12 ‘Worked example III: the coupling count Week 4 deferred’; Session 2, slide 13 ‘What the method cannot tell you’.)*

## Why each check blocks, or only advises

A hard gate stops an entry counting only where the lecture says the failure is a real defect; anything that can occur in good work is an advisory, shown and never blocking. No check tests the form of a name.

## The lab tool

The lab tool imports your team’s Week 4 model-inputs file and adds Parts 19–22. It carries your physical elements, external elements, allocation, information routes, AI capabilities, requirements and risks so that every link can be checked; computes the N² chart with the degree of every element, the trust boundary of every interface and the crossing-flow check (with a relay field for any route that rides more than one interface); and suggests the N² neighbors of a changed interface. Each card, component, interface, requirement and change request lists its hard gates, each marked pass or fails, and its advisories, labeled advisory, which never stop an entry counting; only entries that pass every hard gate count toward the minimums. One-word names, proper nouns, person groups and outside AI services are all valid. One member can enter the record and export the JSON file; teammates import it to continue the same record. Everything is saved in your browser only, so export after every working session.
