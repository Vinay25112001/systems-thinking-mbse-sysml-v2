Week 4 — Architecture / Information View. AeroLink is the instructor’s FICTIONAL worked example; this record is about your team’s own enterprise.

## Purpose

Apply the Week 4 method to your team’s own enterprise, never to AeroLink: choose the viewpoints your stakeholders’ concerns need (at least the operational, functional, physical, information, enterprise views), derive the functions by walking your scenarios and tracing your capabilities, allocate every function to a logical and a physical element with a stated criterion, record one architecture decision, and build the information view that traces each item from its producer to the decision it supports, with an AI capability card. The result extends your Week 3 Team Record with Parts 15–18; Week 5 turns the physical view and the information routes into components and interfaces, and in Phase II the functions, elements and information items become model elements (Weeks 10–12).

## Engineering method

1. **The view set.** Write one viewpoint for each required kind (operational, functional, physical, information, enterprise) and add any view a concern needs. For each: the purpose as the question the view answers, the stakeholders and their concern (in words or as its Week 1 concern ID), the needs, capabilities or requirements it serves where you can name them, and its conventions (the form of the view and the element types it shows). Each entry holds both the viewpoint, the rule, and the view it produces for your one system. Check that every concern in your Week 1 register is answered by at least one view, and that each concern you assign is one the view really answers. Hard gates: a view kind chosen; a purpose: the question the view answers; the stakeholders it serves and their concern, in words or as a concern ID (CON-001); every need, capability or requirement it names exists in the record; its conventions: the form of the view and the element types it shows. Advisory: no need, capability or requirement named: the tool cannot show which of your needs this view helps check. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 4 ‘What an architecture is’; Session 1, slide 6 ‘Framework, model, view, viewpoint: the words, and a gap’; Session 1, slide 7 ‘Choosing views: purpose is paramount’; Session 1, slide 8 ‘AeroLink’s view set, chosen from concerns’.)*
2. **Functions from scenarios.** Walk each Week 2 scenario step by step and ask which transformation each step needs. Name each function as an action, verb and object, never as an element, and give it at least one input and one output, each an information, energy or material flow. A step no function serves is a new function or a recorded gap; a step where a person decides still belongs to a function: write the person in. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 9 ‘What a function is’; Session 1, slide 10 ‘What a functional view must contain’; Session 1, slide 12 ‘Worked example I: walking two scenarios to find functions’.)*
3. **Trace and decomposition.** Trace every function to the capabilities it serves and give any capability without a function a disposition (for example a constraint met by a design property). Group the functions under at least 3 first-level functions beneath your mission function; three levels at most, and stop where a solution is known or imaginable. Hard gates for every function: a name that is not the name of a logical or physical element; at least one input and one output; derived from at least one capability that exists in the record; exercised in a scenario that exists, or marked as a planning function with the reason; placed under a first-level function; every requirement allocated to it exists in the record. Advisories: a one-word name: add the object the action works on (verb and object); the name looks like a thing (system, platform, app, unit, module …): name the action, verb and object; no requirement is allocated to it: fine for a planning function, otherwise check the trace. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 11 ‘Decomposition: how deep, and in which order’; Session 1, slide 13 ‘Functions from capabilities: the coverage check’.)*
4. **Logical and physical elements, allocation.** Define technology-independent logical elements, then the physical elements that implement them in the configuration your Week 3 decision chose (people, procedures and facilities count). Allocate every function to a logical element and to a physical element that logical element reaches, and write one sentence naming the criterion and the ID it rests on. The criteria: similar transformations within the same technology; similar levels of efficiency; exchange of the same type of flow (information, energy or materials); centralized or distributed control; execution at a close frequency level; dependability conditions; environment resistance level; other enterprise constraints. Allocate every system requirement to a function or a physical element. Hard gates: performed by a logical element that exists; allocated to a physical element that exists; the physical element is one its logical element is allocated to; an allocation criterion and a one-sentence justification (at least two words). *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 14 ‘Logical and physical: two structural views’; Session 1, slide 15 ‘Allocation needs a reason: SEBoK’s criteria’; Session 1, slide 16 ‘Two allocation pitfalls’; Session 1, slide 17 ‘Worked example II: function → logical → physical, with the reason’.)*
5. **The architecture decision.** Record at least 1 architecture decision: the question, at least three alternatives considered, the choice, the rationale, and the needs, requirements and risks it touches, with any derived requirement it creates. Hard gates: the architecture question; at least three alternatives considered, one per line; the chosen alternative, which is one of those listed (case, list markers and final punctuation ignored); a rationale; at least one need, requirement or risk it affects that exists in the record. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 18 ‘Recording an architecture decision’; Session 1, slide 20 ‘The process frame: the DoD Architecture Design process’.)*
6. **The information view.** For each information item state the raw data and the information made from them, the producer, the consumer, the decision it supports and the function, requirement or decision record that decision belongs to, how it travels between producer and consumer, and its sensitivity. The consumer is the party that makes the decision: an element of yours, an external system (an alarm to the emergency services, a report to a funder’s portal) or an AI capability; an AI capability can also be a producer, and its card then says who reviews its output. Keep data, decisions and deciders apart: data cross between elements; a decision is made by someone from them. If no decision can be named, it is not information yet. Check that every flow between two of your elements, from your function inputs and outputs, travels in an item. Hard gates: a name and its content; the raw data and the information made from it, each in words, and not the same text; at least one producer that is a physical element, an external element or an AI capability in the record; at least one consumer that is a physical element, an external element or an AI capability in the record; the decision it supports; the function, requirement or decision that decision belongs to, existing in the record. Advisories: say how the item travels from producer to consumer; Week 5 turns that route into an interface; an AI capability produces this item: its card must say who reviews the output before it is used, and what the AI may not decide alone; producer and consumer are the same element: an internal item, not an information flow between elements; the content looks personal (name, address, health, payment …): record its sensitivity. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 3 ‘Data versus information’; Session 2, slide 4 ‘The course method: producer → consumer → decision’; Session 2, slide 5 ‘Information flows check the physical view’; Session 2, slide 8 ‘Worked example I: AeroLink’s information items’.)*
7. **AI capability card.** Write at least 1 AI capability card: the host element, the function it supports, its input information items, its outputs, the human oversight (who decides and what the AI may not do), and its failure modes and their effects, with a note on data quality. Hard gates: hosted on a physical element that exists; supports a function that exists; at least one input information item that exists; its outputs, in words; the human oversight: who decides, and what the AI may not do (“none” is not oversight); at least one failure mode and its effect. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 7 ‘AI capabilities in the information view’; Session 2, slide 10 ‘Worked example II: two AI capability cards’.)*
8. **Consistency and evaluation.** Run the four consistency checks: every function derives from a capability and, unless it is a planning function, from a scenario; every function is allocated to one physical element that its logical element reaches; every system requirement is allocated to a function or a physical element; every information item has a producer, a consumer and the decision it supports. Then evaluate: for each riskiest requirement and TPM, name the element that carries it and the evidence that it can be met, and list what is still open for Week 5. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 11 ‘Auditing the view: two reference defects and one new gap’; Session 2, slide 12 ‘Evaluating the architecture against requirements and TPMs’; Session 2, slide 13 ‘Keeping the views consistent, and common errors’.)*

## The lab tool
