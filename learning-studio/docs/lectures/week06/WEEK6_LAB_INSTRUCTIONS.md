## Engineering method

1. **The as-is and the horizons.** Write the baseline (increment 0) from your Week 4 and Week 5 views: what exists when the enterprise enters service, with the limit that binds it where you can compute one, plus any change already decided and committed, which belongs to the baseline trajectory and is not planned again as an increment. Choose your three horizons, near term, mid term, far term, and state the length of each with a reason: long buckets for long-lived capabilities, short ones for fast-changing ones. Rules: exactly one baseline increment (increment 0); the three horizons each have a stated length (a word or a range is enough: “2 years”). *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 5 ‘Three kinds of capability, and the baseline trajectory’; Session 1, slide 6 ‘Operational needs, capability gaps and excesses over horizons’; Session 2, slide 8 ‘Worked example I: Increment 0, the baseline trajectory’.)*
2. **Capability gaps and excesses.** For each need and objective, compare the desired capability with the baseline trajectory and record what is short (a gap: the baseline below the desired level) or in excess (the baseline above it; slack on a limit that does not bind is not an excess), with both levels, the horizon and the ID it comes from. Hard gates for every entry: what is short or in excess (one word is enough: “Capacity”); the desired level and the baseline level (a number, a word or “TBD” each; at least one of the two stated, not both “TBD” or “none”); gap or excess (a gap: baseline below the desired level; an excess: baseline above it); its horizon: near, mid or far term; the need, objective, capability or requirement behind it: an ID in the record. Advisory: no excess recorded: look for idle capacity as well as gaps. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 6 ‘Operational needs, capability gaps and excesses over horizons’; Session 1, slide 7 ‘From gaps to changes: architecture, opportunities, portfolio’; Session 1, slide 8 ‘The enterprise plan and managing change’.)*
3. **The technology plan.** From your Week 3 readiness assessment, list every technology the roadmap brings to bear: its status (available now, in development, does not yet exist), its TRL with the evidence or labeled assumption behind it, and its insertion point (the increment that brings it in) or the reason it is not inserted. Insert a technology only where maturity, the ability to insert it and the operational timing all hold. Hard gates: its name (one word is enough); its status: available now, in development, or does not yet exist; a TRL from 1 to 9 (not needed when it does not yet exist); the evidence or the labeled assumption behind the TRL (an ID is enough: “A-003”; “TBD” is not a basis); its insertion point: the increment that brings it in, or the reason it is not inserted (one word is enough); if it is immature (below TRL 6, in development or not yet existing): a risk ID or a mitigation (one word is enough). Advisories: inserted in a near-term increment below TRL 6: “no technology before its time”; give the evidence that makes the timing safe, or move it; the TRL differs from your Week 3 readiness assessment: say what new evidence changed it; this TECH ID is not in the Week 3 record you loaded: check the ID, or it is a new technology. Rule: at least one technology inserted by an increment. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 14 ‘What technology planning is’; Session 1, slide 15 ‘The contents of a technology plan’; Session 1, slide 16 ‘Forming the roadmap: the practice steps’; Session 1, slide 17 ‘What maturity evidence can and cannot tell you’; Session 1, slide 19 ‘Worked example: AeroLink’s technology plan’; Session 2, slide 3 ‘A flexible technology roadmap: when to insert’.)*
4. **Increments.** Lay out the increments on the grid of horizons and lanes (capability, technology, architecture and interfaces, operations and people). For each after the baseline give its name, horizon and content; the gap, need or objective it serves; an entry trigger a reviewer could observe; its dependencies; its development approach, one of sequential (requirements well known, familiar domain, manageable risk); incremental (a sequence of increments of growing capability; room for emerging technology); evolutionary (start from a limited set of requirements; each cycle adds value and understanding); agile (iterative and incremental delivery, often combined with the others); its transition approach; its security and resilience change; and an off-ramp wherever it inserts a technology below TRL 6, in development or not yet existing. Hard gates: its name (one word is enough); its horizon: near, mid or far term; its content: what changes (one word is enough); the gap, need or objective it serves: a GAP, NEED, MGO, CAP or REQ ID in the record; its entry trigger: the evidence or event that starts it (one word is enough; a number or an ID is advised; “later” is not a trigger); its development approach: sequential, incremental, evolutionary or agile; its transition approach: how the service keeps operating while it changes (one word is enough: “Cutover”); if it inserts an immature technology (below TRL 6, in development or not yet existing): its off-ramp (one word is enough: “Stay piloted”); its security and resilience change (one word is enough: “None”, with a reason advised); dependencies, if any, name other increments in the record, in the same or an earlier horizon, with no cycle (none is fine). For the baseline: the “as is” baseline in a few words, plus any change already decided (the baseline trajectory; one word is enough). Advisories: the trigger has no number, ID, comparison or event a reviewer could observe: make it measurable where you can; “None” for security and resilience with no reason: say why nothing changes (for example “no new interface”); no off-ramp: fine when every technology it inserts is mature; otherwise consider one; no risk linked: an increment usually changes at least one risk. Rules: at least one increment beyond the near term; advisory, a horizon with no increment: say why nothing changes there, or add one. *(Taught in: Session 1, slide 10 ‘Development approaches: sequential, incremental, evolutionary’; Session 1, slide 11 ‘Evolutionary acquisition: two ways to define increments’; Session 1, slide 12 ‘Five lessons for planning increments’; Session 1, slide 13 ‘Deferral and off-ramps as risk control’; Session 2, slide 7 ‘Anatomy of a roadmap’; Session 2, slide 9 ‘Worked example II: the committed Year-2 step and its transition order’; Session 2, slide 10 ‘Worked example III: Increments 1 to 3, toward resilience and autonomy’; Session 2, slide 11 ‘Worked example IV: triggers, dependencies, and the answer to the board’.)*
5. **Transition while the service operates.** For each increment, plan how the enterprise keeps operating while it changes: simultaneous operation, cutover or retirement; phases by site or area; which limit binds in between; what stays backward compatible; where the change enters the Vee and which unmodified parts are regression tested. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 2 ‘Transition is a process, not a date’; Session 2, slide 4 ‘Modernizing a service or an enterprise while it operates’; Session 2, slide 5 ‘Changing without breaking: the Vee entry point, compatibility, regression’.)*
6. **Security and resilience, and the known unknowns.** State each increment’s security and resilience change; link each new risk to the increment that creates it, including at least one transition risk and at least one security or resilience risk. Record each known unknown with what is unknown, its impact, the information needed, the mitigation, its owner and the increment it gates. Hard gates for each unknown: what is unknown (one word is enough); its impact if it comes out badly (one word is enough); the information needed to close it (one word is enough); the mitigation while it stays open (one word is enough; “none” is not a mitigation); its owner (a role or a person; one word is enough; “TBD” or “?” is not an owner); the increment it gates: an increment in the record; for each AROU item: what it says (one word is enough); for a risk: its category (as in the Week 3 register); its owner (one word is enough); the increment it belongs to: an increment in the record. Advisories: it gates the baseline: fine when the baseline still holds a committed change to be made; otherwise name the later increment it gates; it belongs to the baseline: fine for a committed change still to be made; a transition risk counts only on a later increment. Rules: at least one security or resilience risk linked to an increment (from your register or new); at least one transition risk: a risk that exists only while an increment is being made. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 6 ‘Security and resilience as the enterprise evolves’; Session 1, slide 20 ‘Known unknowns: a managed input to the roadmap’.)*
7. **The impact of one increment.** Choose one increment after the baseline. Follow your record’s links one step from what it inserts or changes (a technology, a stakeholder’s role, an element) and judge each candidate: reached, with how, or not reached, with why. Hard gates: one increment after the baseline; at least one affected ID that exists in your imported record. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 12 ‘Worked example V: an increment’s impact across the package’.)*
8. **The Phase I baseline review.** For each of Weeks 1 to 5, state ready, or name each open item and who closes it, so that Week 7 starts from known open items. Hard gates per week: ready, or open items; if open items: the items and who closes them (one word each is enough). Rule: every ID once. Then draw the roadmap graphic from the grid the tool shows, with its text equivalent. *(Taught in: Session 2, slide 13 ‘The roadmap as a decision instrument, and its limits’; Session 2, slide 14 ‘Preparing the Phase I baseline for Week 7, and what comes after’.)*

## Why each check blocks, or only advises

A hard gate stops an entry counting only where a sentence you can read in the lecture or the lesson says the failure is a real defect; the last column names that slide and quotes the sentence. Anything that can occur in good work is an advisory, shown and never blocking. No check tests the form or length of a name.

A one-word answer counts wherever the lecture calls a word complete, but an answer that says nothing (“n/a”, “none”, “?”, “-”, “later”, “TBD”) never counts where an answer is required: it names no owner, no trigger and no mitigation. The two exceptions are stated in their gates: “None” is a complete security and resilience change, and “TBD” or “none” is a valid capability level, as long as the other level of the same gap is stated.

## The lab tool

The lab tool imports your team’s Week 5 model-inputs file, and optionally your Week 3 file, and adds Parts 23–27. It carries your needs, capabilities, requirements, risks, TPMs, physical elements, functions, components, interfaces and decisions so that every link can be checked; draws the roadmap grid of horizons and increments with its text equivalent; lists, for the increment you choose, the candidates its links reach, so that the impact list starts from the record and not from memory; and seeds the baseline review with one row per week. Each gap, technology, increment, unknown and AROU item lists its hard gates, each marked pass or fails, and its advisories, labeled advisory, which never stop an entry counting; only entries that pass every hard gate count toward the minimums. One-word answers are accepted wherever the lecture calls a word complete (“Cutover”, “None”, “A-003”); an answer that says nothing (“n/a”, “?”, “later”) is not. One member can enter the record and export the JSON file; teammates import it to continue the same record, and the tool asks before replacing work. Everything is saved in your browser only, so export after every working session.
